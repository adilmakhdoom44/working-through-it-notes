Working Through It

How to Keep Working While a Divorce Is Going On

Published 20 November 2026

Sleep goes first. Work goes second, and it goes quietly, which is why most people do not notice until something has already gone wrong. By then it is usually a missed deadline or a meeting that went badly, and the explanation feels too complicated to give.

Nobody plans for this part. You expect the lawyer meetings and the paperwork. You do not expect to read the same email four times and still not know what it says, or to lose a whole afternoon because a message arrived at 11am.

If you are trying to hold a job together while a separation runs in the background, here is what tends to slip and what actually helps.

What goes first at work

Concentration, before anything else. Long stress makes it hard to hold several things in your head at once, which is most of what office work is. People describe it as fog, and they usually blame themselves for it.

Then short term memory. Names, numbers, what was agreed in a meeting an hour ago.

Then patience. A colleague who is slightly annoying on a normal week becomes genuinely difficult on this one. Most people hold it together in front of clients and lose it over something small afterward.

And underneath all of it, time. Not time spent on lawyers, which is visible and plannable, but the twenty minutes that vanish after every message from the other parent. Four of those in a day is most of a morning.

Keep the case out of the working day

The single most effective change is a boundary around when the matter gets attended to.

Pick one slot. Lunchtime, or thirty minutes at the end of the day. Messages get read then, decisions get made then, replies get sent then. Outside the slot the phone is face down and notifications for that channel are off.

This feels irresponsible the first week and then it gives you your day back. Almost nothing in a co-parenting dispute needs an answer inside two hours. The things that genuinely do, like a child being ill at school, arrive as a phone call.

Two practical details make it stick. Put the other parent's messages in their own channel, so you are not forced past them every time you check anything. And keep anything to do with the case off the work laptop and off the work email entirely. Work accounts are not private, they belong to the employer, and nobody wants their custody correspondence sitting on a company server.

What to tell your employer

Most people either say nothing at all or say far too much to the wrong person.

Saying nothing is usually a mistake if the situation is going to run for months. From the outside, unexplained lateness and missed deadlines look like someone who has stopped caring. A manager who knows there is something going on reads exactly the same behavior completely differently.

You do not owe anyone the story. One line is enough. I am dealing with a family matter that will take a few months and may need some time off for appointments. I will give you as much notice as I can.

Say it to the person who needs to plan around it, usually your direct manager, and not to the whole team. Details shared at work travel, and in a contested case anything you say can come back in a form you did not intend.

If your workplace has a confidential support line, it is worth knowing it exists before you need it rather than after.

Court dates and appointments

Hearings get moved, often at short notice, which is a planning problem as much as an emotional one.

Ask for the whole day rather than the morning. Even a short hearing eats the day, and going back to the office afterward to be useful almost never works out.

Book the time off as soon as a date exists, and keep a simple list of the dates somewhere your employer can be told about in one message rather than five.

Build in the day after as well if you can. The flat, exhausted day that follows a hearing is a real thing, and it is better spent somewhere other than a meeting room.

The overwork trap

There is a specific pattern worth naming. Money gets tighter, legal costs arrive, and the obvious response is to work more. On top of that, work is often the one place that still feels under control, which makes it a comfortable place to hide.

For a few weeks it works. After that it stops working, usually right when you need to be sharp for something that matters.

If the children are with the other parent two weekends a month, those weekends are not free capacity. Filling them with work is how people arrive at the next hearing with nothing left.

When it is showing up in your body

The signs that this has gone past a hard patch tend to appear at work before anywhere else, because work is where the demands are.

Worth taking seriously: weeks of being unable to concentrate rather than days, regular headaches, chest tightness, appetite gone or doubled, catching every illness going round, or needing a drink most evenings to switch off.

People in the middle of a separation explain all of that away as stress and put off seeing anyone for months. It is worth getting checked, and it is worth saying plainly what is going on, because the context matters to whoever is assessing you.

Protect the first hour of the day

One small habit does more than the rest of this list put together. Do not open messages from the other parent before work.

A message read at 7am sets the tone for the entire day, and you are at your least able to decide what to do about it. Read them in the slot, with the day already underway, when you can judge whether something needs a reply at all.

The same goes for the evening. Reading at eleven at night buys a bad night's sleep, which buys a worse day tomorrow, and the whole thing compounds.

The underlying fix

Most of the disruption at work comes from unpredictability. Not knowing what is coming, not knowing what to do about the last thing, and carrying both around all day.

Which is why the thing that helps most is not a productivity system. It is having the arrangement tightened up so fewer things arrive unexpectedly, and having decided in advance what you respond to and what you let sit.

That is the practical side of practical coaching through a high conflict separation, as distinct from therapy and from legal advice. Not how you feel about the marriage, and not what the law says. What to do with the message that came in at 11am, so it does not take the rest of the day with it.

Start with the slot. One fixed window, phone face down outside it. It is a small change and it is the one that gives the working day back.
